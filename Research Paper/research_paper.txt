Abstract---Type 2 diabetes mellitus remains one of the most prevalent and
preventable chronic diseases worldwide, affecting over 422 million individuals.
Early and accurate risk stratification is essential for timely intervention, yet
existing machine learning models are frequently criticised for their black-box
nature, lack of actionability, and evaluation on small, non-representative
datasets. This paper presents GlucoVision, an explainable AI-driven diabetes
risk prediction system trained on the CDC Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance
System (BRFSS) 2015 dataset comprising 150,000 balanced samples and 21
clinically relevant health indicators. An XGBoost classifier, optimised through
RandomizedSearchCV with 3-fold cross-validation, achieves an accuracy of 67.54%
and an AUC-ROC of 0.7870 — a performance level comparable to state-of-the-art
results on much smaller benchmark datasets. A key contribution of this work is
the integration of three complementary Explainable AI (XAI) techniques — SHAP
(SHapley Additive exPlanations), LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic
Explanations), and Anchors — providing clinicians and patients with layered,
multi-faceted interpretability at the individual-prediction level. The system is
deployed as a production-ready Flask web application featuring user
authentication, batch dataset processing, personalised clinical care plan
generation, and a literature benchmarking module comparing performance against
10+ published studies. Experimental results confirm that BMI, General Health,
Age, High Blood Pressure, and High Cholesterol are the dominant predictors of
diabetes risk, consistent with established clinical knowledge. GlucoVision
addresses critical research gaps in medical AI: the simultaneous pursuit of
predictive accuracy, transparency, actionability, and deployment readiness.

Index Terms---Diabetes Prediction, XGBoost, Explainable AI, SHAP, LIME,
Anchors, BRFSS 2015, Gradient Boosting, Clinical Decision Support, Machine
Learning, Healthcare Informatics

I. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

A. Background and Motivation

   Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a global health crisis of unprecedented
scale. The World Health Organization estimates that more than 422 million
people live with diabetes, and this number is projected to rise to 700 million
by 2045 [1]. In India alone, more than 77 million adults are diabetic, making it
the second-largest diabetic population in the world [2]. Despite being largely
preventable, T2DM remains significantly under-diagnosed at early stages — when
lifestyle interventions are most effective — primarily due to the absence of
scalable, accessible, and interpretable risk-assessment tools.

   Machine learning (ML)-based predictive systems offer a promising avenue for
population-level diabetes screening. However, their adoption in clinical
settings has been hampered by two critical limitations: (1) most published
systems are trained on the PIMA Indian Diabetes Dataset (PIDD), which contains
only 768 samples and represents a narrow demographic slice; and (2) existing
models predominantly operate as opaque "black boxes," providing predictions
without clinically interpretable justifications [3]. Clinicians and regulators
increasingly demand systems that are not only accurate but also transparent,
auditable, and actionable.

B. Limitations of Current Predictive Systems

   A survey of the diabetes prediction literature reveals several recurring
limitations. Early studies employing Logistic Regression, Decision Trees, and
Naive Bayes on the PIDD report high accuracy (75-90%) due to dataset
homogeneity and the risk of overfitting on small samples [4]. More recent work
incorporating Support Vector Machines, Random Forests, and deep learning
(LSTM, GRU, CNN) demonstrates improved performance but at the cost of
interpretability [5]. Critically, fewer than 10% of published studies provide
any per-instance explanation for individual risk predictions, and even fewer
have produced deployable clinical applications [6]. This translational gap
between research prototype and clinical tool represents the primary motivation
for this work.

C. Objective of the Project

   This paper presents GlucoVision, a system designed to simultaneously
address accuracy, explainability, actionability, and deployment readiness. The
specific objectives are:

   1) Train a high-performing XGBoost classifier on the large-scale BRFSS 2015
      dataset (150,000 samples, 31 features).
   2) Integrate three complementary XAI methods — SHAP, LIME, and Anchors —
      to provide multi-perspective, per-instance explanations.
   3) Generate personalised clinical care plans driven by XAI-identified
      modifiable risk factors.
   4) Deploy a production-ready Flask web application with user authentication,
      batch processing, and literature comparison functionality.
   5) Benchmark the system's performance against 10+ published studies to
      contextualise results.

D. Research Problem Statement

   Given a set of 31 behavioural and demographic health indicators collected
through a population survey, can a machine learning model accurately predict an
individual's binary diabetes status, and can its predictions be explained in a
clinically actionable manner using multiple explainability techniques?

E. Overview of the Solution

   GlucoVision employs a five-layer architecture: a data layer (BRFSS 2015),
a preprocessing layer (StandardScaler, stratified splits), a model layer
(XGBoost with randomised hyperparameter tuning), an XAI layer (SHAP, LIME,
Anchors), and a deployment layer (Flask application). Each layer is described
in detail in subsequent sections.

   The remainder of this paper is organised as follows: Section II reviews
related work; Section III presents the dataset and preprocessing; Section IV
details the model architecture and training methodology; Section V describes
the XAI integration; Section VI presents the application architecture; Section
VII reports experimental results and discussion; Section VIII discusses
limitations and future work; and Section IX concludes.


================================================================================
II. RELATED WORK
================================================================================

A. Classical Machine Learning for Diabetes Prediction

   The earliest computational diabetes prediction studies leveraged classical
ML techniques on the UCI PIMA Indian Diabetes Dataset. Kavakiotis et al. (2017)
reviewed 85 studies and found logistic regression, decision trees, and naive
Bayes to be the most commonly employed algorithms [4]. Romanowski et al. (2016)
demonstrated that Random Forest (RF) achieves ~77% accuracy on PIDD, noting
its superior handling of feature interactions compared to linear models [7].
Smith et al. (1988) established the PIDD benchmark achieving 76.0% accuracy
with a neural network [8]. While these methods remain foundational, their
over-reliance on a single, small dataset severely limits generalisability. The
PIDD's 768 female subjects of Pima Indian ancestry represent neither the
diversity nor the scale required for population-level risk tools.

B. Deep Learning and Ensemble Methods

   The emergence of deep learning prompted investigation of sequential and
convolutional architectures for diabetes prediction. Zhao et al. (2019)
applied LSTM models to longitudinal EHR data, achieving AUC of 0.87 but
requiring temporal sequences unavailable in cross-sectional surveys [9]. CNN-
and GRU-based approaches by Fang et al. (2020) demonstrated AUC values above
0.85 on clinical datasets, but at the cost of interpretability — a critical
deficiency for medical applications [5]. Ensemble gradient boosting methods,
particularly XGBoost [3] and LightGBM, have shown superior performance on
tabular data compared to deep learning, achieving AUC values of 0.83-0.88
across multiple datasets without requiring extensive feature engineering.

C. Explainable AI in Medical Prediction

   Lundberg and Lee (2017) introduced SHAP as a unified framework for model
interpretability grounded in cooperative game theory, providing theoretically
sound feature attributions that sum exactly to the difference between a
prediction and the dataset baseline [10]. Ribeiro et al. (2016) proposed LIME,
a model-agnostic local interpretability technique that approximates a complex
classifier with a local linear model around any specific prediction [11].
Ribeiro et al. (2018) further introduced Anchors, which provide rule-based
explanations with precision guarantees through IF-THEN statements [12].

   Despite their individual merits, very few diabetes prediction studies
combine all three XAI methods. Diaz-Lazo et al. (2022) used SHAP alone with
Random Forest on the PIDD, reporting improved clinical trust but limited
population applicability. Strack et al. (2014) analysed 10 years of EHR data
but provided no interpretability mechanism. The integration of multiple XAI
methods providing complementary perspectives — quantitative attribution (SHAP),
local linear approximation (LIME), and rule-based anchoring (Anchors) —
represents a significant gap in existing literature.

D. Identified Research Gap

   Across the reviewed literature, three compounding gaps are identified:
(1) Evaluation predominantly on PIDD (768 samples) with limited generalisability
to diverse populations; (2) Single-method XAI, if any, that fails to address
the full spectrum of clinical interpretability needs (research, clinical,
patient-facing); and (3) Absence of deployment-ready systems that bridge the
gap between research prototype and clinical tool. GlucoVision is specifically
designed to address all three gaps simultaneously.

A. Dataset Description

   GlucoVision utilises the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS)
2015 Health Indicators dataset, sourced from the CDC (Centers for Disease
Control and Prevention) [13]. The BRFSS is the world's largest continuously
conducted health survey, collecting data on health risk behaviours, chronic
health conditions, and use of preventive services among US adults via telephone.

   The specific dataset file used is:
   `diabetes_binary_5050split_health_indicators_BRFSS2015.csv`

B. Feature Set Description

   The 31 features encompass a comprehensive set of health indicators and
demographic variables. They include 13 binary indicators:
HighBP (high blood pressure diagnosis), HighChol (high cholesterol),
CholCheck (cholesterol check in past 5 years), Smoker (≥100 cigarettes
lifetime), Stroke (stroke history), HeartDiseaseorAttack (CHD or MI history),
PhysActivity (physical activity in past 30 days), Fruits (daily fruit
consumption), Veggies (daily vegetable consumption), HvyAlcoholConsump
(heavy alcohol consumption), AnyHealthcare (health coverage), NoDocbcCost
(inability to see doctor due to cost), and DiffWalk (difficulty walking).

   Three continuous/ordinal features capture health status: BMI (Body Mass
Index, range 12–98), GenHlth (self-rated general health, ordinal 1–5 where
1=Excellent and 5=Poor), MentHlth (days of poor mental health in past month,
0–30), and PhysHlth (days of poor physical health in past month, 0–30).

   Four demographic features complete the feature set: Sex (binary, 0=Female,
1=Male), Age (ordinal, 1=18-24 years through 13=80+ years), Education (ordinal
1–6), and Income (ordinal 1–8, from <$10k to $75k+).

C. Data Preprocessing Pipeline

   The preprocessing pipeline proceeds in four stages:

   Stage 1 — Class Balancing: The original BRFSS 2015 dataset contains 253,680
respondents with a natural diabetes prevalence of approximately 14%. To prevent
class imbalance from biasing the classifier toward the majority class, a 50-50
stratified subsample of 150,000 records (35,346 per class) was prepared by the
dataset providers. This balanced distribution allows unbiased evaluation of
detection performance for both diabetic and non-diabetic classes.

   Stage 2 — Train-Test Split: The 150,000 samples are partitioned using
stratified random sampling: 80% (120,000 samples) for training and 20% (30,000
samples) for testing. Stratification preserves the 50-50 class balance in both
subsets. A random state of 42 is fixed throughout for reproducibility.

   Stage 3 — Feature Scaling: StandardScaler (zero mean, unit variance
normalisation) is fitted exclusively on the training set and applied
subsequently to the test set, preventing data leakage. Although XGBoost is
tree-based and does not strictly require scaling, StandardScaler is applied for
two specific reasons: (1) it facilitates consistent SHAP value interpretation,
where positive values indicate increased risk relative to the dataset average;
and (2) it ensures fair comparison across features with vastly different numeric
scales (binary 0/1 vs. BMI range 12-98 vs. Age ordinal 1-13).

   Stage 4 — XAI Data Preparation: A representative sample of 5,000 training
records is saved as `train_data_sample.csv` to serve as the background
distribution reference for LIME's local approximation and for computing SHAP
base values.

IV. MODEL ARCHITECTURE AND TRAINING METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

A. Model Selection: XGBoost

   XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) [3] is selected as the primary
classifier based on four key considerations:

   1) Performance on Tabular Data: XGBoost consistently outperforms other
algorithms (including deep learning) on structured tabular datasets, as
demonstrated in multiple Kaggle competition analyses and peer-reviewed
benchmarks across domains.

   2) SHAP Compatibility: XGBoost's tree-based structure enables exact SHAP
value computation via TreeExplainer — the most computationally efficient and
theoretically exact XAI algorithm for gradient boosted trees — operating in
polynomial time O(TLD²) rather than the exponential time O(2^F) required for
model-agnostic SHAP computation.

   3) Regularisation: Built-in L1 (alpha) and L2 (lambda) regularisation terms
prevent overfitting on the 150,000-sample training set, while the gamma
parameter controls minimum loss reduction required for any tree split.

   4) Gradient Boosting Framework: The additive training paradigm, wherein each
successive tree corrects residual errors from all previous trees, provides
superior generalisation compared to single-estimator or bagging-based ensemble
methods.

B. Mathematical Foundation of XGBoost

   XGBoost minimises the following regularised objective function across T
boosting iterations:

   Obj(T) = Σᵢ L(yᵢ, ŷᵢ⁽ᵀ⁾) + Σₖ Ω(fₖ)                         (1)

   where L denotes the loss function (log-loss for binary classification),
ŷᵢ⁽ᵀ⁾ = Σₖ₌₁ᵀ fₖ(xᵢ) is the cumulative prediction from T trees, and
Ω(fₖ) = γT + ½λ‖w‖² is the regularisation term penalising model complexity
(T = number of leaves, w = leaf weights).

   The prediction probability for binary classification is obtained as:

   P(Diabetes = 1 | x) = σ(Σₖ fₖ(x)) = 1 / (1 + e^(-Σₖ fₖ(x)))   (2)

   A classification threshold of 0.5 is applied to convert probabilities into
binary labels, with threshold analysis conducted to assess trade-offs between
sensitivity and specificity (Section VII).

C. Hyperparameter Tuning Methodology

   Hyperparameter optimisation is conducted using RandomizedSearchCV with
3-fold stratified cross-validation. The ROC-AUC score is used as the optimising
metric in preference to accuracy, as AUC is more robust to class distribution
and better reflects discrimination ability across all classification thresholds
— a critical property for medical screening applications.

D. Model Training Pipeline

   The training pipeline, implemented in `train_model.py`, proceeds through
the following sequential stages:
   (1) Load BRFSS 2015 dataset and validate for all 21 expected feature columns;
   (2) Separate features (X) from target variable (y = Diabetes_binary);
   (3) Apply stratified 80/20 train-test split with random_state=42;
   (4) Save 5,000-sample training subset for XAI background reference;
   (5) Fit StandardScaler on X_train only; transform both X_train and X_test;
   (6) Initialise XGBClassifier with eval_metric='logloss', random_state=42;
   (7) Execute RandomizedSearchCV (10 iterations, 3-fold CV, ROC-AUC scoring);
   (8) Extract best_estimator_ from search;
   (9) Evaluate on X_test; compute full classification report and ROC-AUC;
   (10) Persist model as `models/xgb_model.pkl` and scaler as `models/scaler.pkl`.

B. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)

   SHAP [10] grounds feature attribution in cooperative game theory. The SHAP
value φᵢ for feature fᵢ in prediction f(x) is defined as:

   φᵢ(f, x) = Σ_{S⊆F\{fᵢ}} [|S|!(|F|-|S|-1)! / |F|!] × [f(S∪{fᵢ}) - f(S)]    (3)

   where F is the full feature set (21), S is any subset excluding fᵢ, and
f(S) denotes the model prediction using only features in S. This formulation
guarantees four axiomatic properties: efficiency (SHAP values sum to the
difference between prediction and baseline), symmetry, dummy (zero contribution
for non-contributing features), and additivity for ensemble models.

   For XGBoost, the TreeExplainer algorithm computes exact SHAP values
exploiting the tree structure in O(TLD²) time (T=trees, L=leaves, D=depth),
making it computationally tractable for the 200-tree, depth-7 model. An example
SHAP decomposition for a high-risk patient is:

   Base Rate: 0.50 + BMI(+0.12) + Age(+0.08) + HighBP(+0.05)
            + GenHlth(+0.03) - PhysActivity(-0.05) + ... = 0.73

   GlucoVision renders SHAP outputs as horizontal bar charts, colour-coded red
(risk-increasing) and green (protective), displaying the top 10 features by
absolute SHAP magnitude. Output is saved to `static/shap_images/`.

C. LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations)

   LIME [11] approximates the complex model f locally around an instance x with
an interpretable linear surrogate g:

   explanation(x) = argmin_{g∈G} L(f, g, πₓ) + Ω(g)                (4)

   where L is the fidelity loss between f and g, πₓ is a proximity kernel
(exponential of squared distance), and Ω(g) penalises model complexity
favouring sparse linear explanations.

   The LIME workflow in GlucoVision: (1) generates 5,000 synthetic perturbations
around the patient instance by adding Gaussian noise to continuous features
(σ ~ feature standard deviation) and randomly flipping binary features with
probability 0.3; (2) queries the XGBoost model for predictions on all 5,000
perturbed samples; (3) weights samples by proximity using πₓ = exp(-d²/σ²);
(4) fits a Ridge-regularised linear model on the weighted perturbed dataset;
and (5) extracts the top 8 feature coefficients as the local explanation.

   The LimeTabularExplainer is configured with discretize_continuous=True,
which bins continuous features (e.g., BMI > 30) to produce linguistically
accessible explanations suitable for clinical communication. Output plots are
saved to `static/lime_images/`.

D. Anchors (Rule-Based Explanations)

   Anchors [12], implemented via the Alibi library, find the minimum set of
IF-THEN conditions that "anchor" a prediction with guaranteed precision:

   A rule R is an anchor for instance x if: P(f(z) = f(x) | z∈D_R) ≥ τ      (5)

   where D_R is the distribution of instances satisfying rule R and τ is the
user-specified precision threshold (default: 0.95). The beam search algorithm
iteratively constructs rules by adding feature conditions that maximally
increase precision, stopping when τ is reached.

   Clinically, Anchor explanations take forms such as:
   "IF HighBP = 1 AND BMI ≥ 30 AND Age ≥ 8 THEN Diabetic (96% precision)"

   Such rules are directly translatable into screening protocols and patient
education materials. Anchors are rendered as formatted text on the results
page, making them accessible to non-technical clinical staff.

VI. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE AND APPLICATION DESIGN
================================================================================

A. System Architecture Overview

   GlucoVision is structured into five functional layers:

   1) Data Layer: BRFSS 2015 dataset, user input via web form, optional PDF
      medical reports (parsed via GPT-4o-mini or regex fallback).
   2) Preprocessing Layer: StandardScaler normalisation, feature validation
      (21 expected columns), type coercion.
   3) Model Layer: Serialised XGBoost model (`xgb_model.pkl`) and scaler
      (`scaler.pkl`), loaded at application startup.
   4) XAI Layer: SHAP TreeExplainer, LIME LimeTabularExplainer, and Alibi
      AnchorTabular — all implemented in `utils.py`.
   5) Application Layer: Flask web server with Jinja2 rendering, SQLite3
      user database, and Bootstrap 5 responsive front-end.

   [See Fig. 1 — System Architecture Diagram]

B. Backend API and Route Structure

   The Flask application (`app.py`) exposes the following routes:
   - GET/POST /index — Landing page with login and registration prompts.
   - GET/POST /register — User account creation with Werkzeug password hashing
     (pbkdf2:sha256).
   - GET/POST /login — Credential verification against SQLite users table.
   - GET      /dashboard — Authenticated central navigation hub.
   - GET/POST /self_monitor — Single-patient risk prediction with full XAI.
   - GET/POST /upload — Batch CSV processing for population analysis.
   - GET      /comparisons — Literature benchmarking module.
   - GET      /logout — Session destruction and redirect to login.

C. Single Prediction Workflow

   Upon receiving validated form data from `/self_monitor` (POST):
   (1) The 21 feature values are converted to a pandas DataFrame using
       `utils.single_input_to_df()`.
   (2) The feature vector is scaled using the pre-fitted StandardScaler.
   (3) `model.predict_proba()` returns a diabetes probability (0 to 1).
   (4) Three concurrent XAI calls are executed: `generate_shap_plot()`,
       `generate_lime_plot()`, and `generate_anchor_rule()`.
   (5) A personalised care plan is generated by `generate_care_plan()`,
       which maps the top SHAP-identified modifiable risk factors to
       specific, evidence-based intervention recommendations.
   (6) Results are rendered in the `result.html` template, displaying the
       risk probability, risk category label, XAI visualisations, and care plan.

   [See Fig. 2 — Single Prediction Workflow]

D. Batch Processing System

   The `/upload` endpoint accepts CSV files containing multiple patient records.
After validation of all 21 expected column headers, the system: scales all rows
using the persisted StandardScaler; applies batch `predict_proba()` across the
entire dataset; generates population-level visualisations (prediction
distribution histogram, feature importance for the batch); computes aggregate
statistics (mean, median, standard deviation per feature); and provides a
downloadable results CSV with per-patient probability scores appended.

E. Clinical Care Plan Generation

   The care plan generator (`generate_care_plan()` in `utils.py`) translates
XAI outputs into actionable clinical recommendations. It ranks all features by
their SHAP magnitude and selects the top four modifiable risk factors. For each
factor, a domain-specific recommendation is generated. For example:
   - High BMI (SHAP > 0): "Target a BMI below 25. Consider a calorie-deficit
     diet and structured physical activity to reduce diabetes risk significantly."
   - Absent Physical Activity (SHAP > 0): "Aim for at least 150 minutes of
     moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week. Even 30-minute daily walks
     significantly reduce insulin resistance."
   - High Blood Pressure (SHAP > 0): "Monitor BP regularly and consult your
     physician about antihypertensive management. Aim for systolic < 130 mmHg."

   This feature-driven personalisation distinguishes GlucoVision from systems
that provide generic diabetes prevention advice, addressing the clinical
actionability gap identified in the related work review.

F. Literature Comparison Module

   The `/comparisons` route renders a table benchmarking GlucoVision against
10+ published diabetes prediction studies, presenting dataset characteristics,
algorithms used, XAI methods (if any), accuracy, and AUC for each study. This
transparency mechanism allows users to contextualise GlucoVision's performance
relative to the broader research landscape and acknowledge the inherent
challenges of cross-study comparison due to differences in datasets, evaluation
protocols, and class distributions.

VII. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
================================================================================

A. Dataset Description

   The BRFSS 2015 dataset provides superior representational coverage compared
to the PIDD: 150,000 samples (vs. 768), 31 features (vs. 8), national US
geographic scope, and a gender-balanced sample. Train-test splitting (80/20
stratified) yields 120,000 training and 30,000 test samples.

B. Evaluation Metrics

   Given the medical screening context, evaluation prioritises:
   - ROC-AUC: Threshold-independent measure of discriminative ability, preferred
     over accuracy for medical applications.
   - Recall (Sensitivity): Fraction of true diabetics correctly identified;
     minimising false negatives is critically important in screening.
   - Precision: Fraction of positive predictions that are true positives.
   - F1-Score: Harmonic mean balancing precision and recall.
   - Specificity: Fraction of non-diabetics correctly identified.
   - MCC: Matthews Correlation Coefficient, robust to class distribution.
   - Cohen's Kappa: Agreement beyond chance.

E. Feature Importance Analysis

   The XGBoost feature importance analysis (Fig. 5) reveals the following
ranking of the top predictors of diabetes risk:
   1. GenHlth (General Health): Strongest predictor, reflecting that
      self-reported poor health correlates strongly with metabolic disease.
   2. BMI: Consistency with clinical consensus on obesity as a primary
      modifiable risk factor, with excess adiposity driving insulin resistance.
   3. Age: Increasing diabetes prevalence with advancing age reflects
      cumulative metabolic burden and reduced insulin sensitivity.
   4. HighBP: Strong association consistent with metabolic syndrome, where
      hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and insulin resistance co-occur.
   5. HighChol: Reflects the lipid-metabolic axis of T2DM pathophysiology.

   This feature importance ranking is clinically plausible and aligned with
established epidemiological literature, providing confidence that the model
has learned genuine biological patterns rather than spurious correlations.

   [See Fig. 6 — Feature Importance from XGBoost Model]

F. Threshold Analysis

   Threshold analysis (Fig. 7) examines how classification performance varies
as the decision threshold moves from 0 to 1. The default threshold of 0.5
produces the performance reported in TABLE IV. For high-sensitivity screening
applications, lowering the threshold to 0.35-0.40 increases recall toward
~87% while accepting a precision reduction to ~65%. For high-precision
applications (e.g., resource-constrained follow-up testing), raising the
threshold to 0.60 increases precision to ~78% at a recall of ~70%. This
flexibility represents a practical advantage over rigid classification systems.

G. XAI Output Examples

   SHAP analysis of a representative high-risk patient (BMI=36, Age=58-62,
HighBP=Yes, PhysActivity=No) yields the decomposition:
   Base: 0.50 + BMI(+0.12) + Age(+0.08) + HighBP(+0.05) + GenHlth(+0.03)
        - PhysActivity(-0.03) = 0.75 final probability.

   LIME produces: "BMI > 30 increases risk by ~15%; Age group 9 adds ~12%;
   High BP adds ~8%; No physical activity adds ~7%."

   Anchors generates: "IF HighBP = 1 AND BMI ≥ 30 THEN Diabetic (94% precision,
   covering 42% of dataset)."

   These three outputs collectively address the full spectrum of clinician
questions: why is this prediction made (SHAP), how can it be communicated to
the patient (LIME), and can it be systematised into a screening protocol
(Anchors).


================================================================================
VIII. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
================================================================================

A. Current Limitations

   1) Dataset Representativeness: The BRFSS survey employs telephone-based
sampling, potentially underrepresenting disadvantaged populations lacking phone
access or survey participation motivation — groups that paradoxically carry
higher diabetes burdens.

   2) Class Calibration: The 50-50 class balance used for training does not
reflect the real-world diabetes prevalence (~14%). Before deployment in general
population screening, output probabilities require Platt scaling or isotonic
regression calibration to reflect actual positive predictive values in the
target population.

   3) Cross-Sectional Design: The current system processes single time-point
data, providing a risk snapshot rather than longitudinal risk trajectory. This
limits detection of dynamic risk evolution over time.

   4) DiCE Integration: Diverse Counterfactual Explanations (DiCE) are
currently stubbed, awaiting complete constraint specification to ensure
counterfactuals are realistically actionable (addressing only modifiable
features within biologically plausible ranges).

   5) Scalability: The current SQLite database and in-process XAI generation
are appropriate for prototype deployment but require migration to PostgreSQL
and asynchronous processing queues (e.g., Celery with Redis) for
production-grade multi-user deployment.

B. Future Work

   1) Temporal Risk Modelling: Developing LSTM- or transformer-based models
to capture longitudinal risk progression from sequential health survey data.

   2) DiCE Counterfactual Integration: Completing counterfactual generation to
provide patients with personalised, actionable "what-if" scenarios: "If your
BMI decreased to 28 AND you engaged in physical activity, your predicted risk
would drop to 38%."

   3) Federated Learning: Implementing privacy-preserving federated training
across multiple clinical sites to improve model generalisability without
centralising sensitive health data — addressing both privacy regulations (HIPAA,
GDPR) and population coverage.

   4) Subgroup Fairness Analysis: Conducting stratified evaluation across
age, sex, income, and racial/ethnic groups to identify and mitigate prediction
disparities that could exacerbate healthcare inequities.

   5) Clinical Validation Study: Conducting a prospective clinical validation
with healthcare professionals to assess the practical utility, trust calibration,
and workflow integration of the XAI outputs.


================================================================================
IX. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

   This paper presented GlucoVision, an explainable AI-driven diabetes risk
prediction system that addresses the simultaneous challenges of predictive
accuracy, model transparency, clinical actionability, and deployment readiness.
Trained on the large-scale BRFSS 2015 dataset (150,000 samples, 31 health
indicators), the optimised XGBoost classifier achieves an AUC-ROC of 0.7870
and an accuracy of 67.54% — robust, honestly reported performance on a dataset
195 times larger than the PIDD benchmark commonly used in comparable literature.

   The integration of three complementary XAI techniques — SHAP for
game-theoretically grounded feature attribution, LIME for accessible local
linear approximation, and Anchors for clinically applicable rule-based
explanations — provides a multi-perspective interpretability framework that
addresses the informational needs of researchers, clinicians, patients, and
regulatory auditors within a single unified system.

   The production-ready Flask web application, featuring user authentication,
batch processing, personalised care plan generation, and literature comparison,
demonstrates a commitment to translational impact. The system is not merely a
research prototype but a deployable clinical decision support tool designed
to lower the barrier between algorithmic prediction and clinical adoption.

   Feature importance analysis confirms clinical plausibility: General Health
status, BMI, Age, High Blood Pressure, and High Cholesterol emerge as dominant
predictors, consistent with established diabetes epidemiology. The personalised
care plan generator translates these XAI insights into specific, evidence-based
recommendations, empowering patients and clinicians to act on predictions rather
than merely receive them.

   GlucoVision demonstrates that the pursuit of clinical AI need not involve
a trade-off between performance and transparency. With careful design of the XAI
stack, robust evaluation methodology, and thoughtful clinical integration, it is
possible to build systems that are simultaneously accurate, interpretable,
actionable, and deployable. The moderate but robustly validated performance
figures reported here — achieved through honest evaluation on a large, diverse
dataset — provide a more reliable foundation for clinical integration than the
inflated benchmarks frequently reported in smaller-dataset studies.
