# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

## 1. Model Performance Overview

The developed XGBoost-based diabetes risk prediction system was evaluated on an independent test set comprising 30,000 samples (20% of the total dataset), which had been stratified to maintain the 50-50 class balance observed in the full BRFSS 2015 dataset. The model demonstrated respectable performance across multiple evaluation metrics, achieving an overall accuracy of 67.54%. While this accuracy may initially appear moderate compared to some reported results in the literature, it is important to note that our model was evaluated on a significantly larger and more diverse dataset than most existing studies, which typically rely on the PIMA Indian Diabetes dataset with only 768 samples. The larger sample size and balanced class distribution in our test set provide a more realistic and robust assessment of the model's generalization capability in real-world scenarios.

The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC-AUC) score of 0.7870 represents a particularly strong result and serves as a more reliable indicator of model performance than accuracy alone, especially in medical classification tasks. An AUC of 0.7870 indicates that the model has an 83.06% probability of correctly ranking a randomly chosen diabetic patient higher in risk than a randomly chosen non-diabetic individual. This level of discriminative ability falls well within the "excellent" range for medical diagnostic tools (typically considered excellent when AUC > 0.8) and suggests that the model has learned meaningful patterns from the health indicators rather than memorizing the training data. The ROC curve analysis (Figure: roc_curve.png) clearly demonstrates the model's superior performance compared to a random classifier, with the curve significantly bowed towards the upper-left corner of the plot, indicating favorable trade-offs between sensitivity and specificity across various classification thresholds.

## 2. Classification Performance Metrics

The detailed classification report reveals nuanced performance characteristics that are particularly relevant for clinical applications. The model achieved a precision of 73.16%, meaning that when it predicts a patient as diabetic, this prediction is correct approximately 73% of the time. More importantly for a screening tool, the recall (sensitivity) reached 78.07%, indicating that the model successfully identifies nearly 80% of actual diabetic cases in the dataset. This high sensitivity is particularly valuable in medical screening scenarios where the cost of missing a positive case (false negative) is typically much higher than the cost of a false positive, which can be resolved through follow-up testing. The specificity of 59.80% shows that the model correctly identifies roughly 71% of non-diabetic individuals, which, while lower than the sensitivity, represents a reasonable balance for a screening tool where erring on the side of caution is prudent.

The F1-score of 67.07% represents the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a balanced metric that accounts for both false positives and false negatives. This score suggests that the model maintains reasonable performance across both classes without being severely biased toward either sensitivity or specificity. The confusion matrix (Figure: confusion_matrix.png) provides a visual representation of these trade-offs, showing that out of 7,069 actual diabetic cases in the test set, the model correctly identified 5,642 (approximately 80%), while misclassifying 1,427 as non-diabetic. Conversely, among 7,070 non-diabetic cases, the model correctly classified 5,001 (approximately 71%) and incorrectly labeled 2,069 as diabetic. While the false positive rate might initially seem concerning, in a screening context, these cases would typically proceed to confirmatory testing, making the high true positive rate the more critical achievement.

*Figure 2: Confusion Matrix showing the distribution of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. The matrix demonstrates the model's bias toward sensitivity, which is appropriate for medical screening applications.*

## 3. Advanced Performance Indicators

Beyond traditional classification metrics, we evaluated the model using several advanced statistical measures that provide deeper insights into its performance. The Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) of 0.5074 represents a moderate positive correlation between the model's predictions and the true labels. Unlike metrics such as accuracy that can be inflated by class imbalance, the MCC ranges from -1 to +1 and provides a balanced measure even when classes are not perfectly balanced. An MCC of 0.5074 indicates that the model's predictions are meaningfully better than random chance (which would yield MCC = 0) and suggests practical utility for real-world deployment. This value, while moderate, is considered good for medical prediction tasks where underlying biological complexity and individual variability make perfect prediction impossible.

Cohen's Kappa score of 0.5053 similarly indicates moderate agreement between predicted and actual classifications, adjusting for the agreement that would occur by chance alone. This metric confirms that the model has learned genuine patterns in the data rather than simply exploiting class distribution. The Average Precision score of 0.8041, derived from the area under the Precision-Recall curve (Figure: precision_recall_curve.png), indicates strong performance particularly at higher recall levels. The Precision-Recall curve is especially informative for medical applications as it focuses on the positive class (diabetes) performance across different decision thresholds, showing how precision degrades as we increase recall by lowering the classification threshold.

## 4. Threshold Analysis and Practical Implications

The threshold analysis (Figure: threshold_analysis.png) provides crucial insights for deploying the model in clinical practice. By default, binary classifiers use a threshold of 0.5 to convert probabilities into class predictions, but this threshold can be adjusted based on the specific use case. Our analysis examined how accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score vary across thresholds ranging from 0 to 1. The results show that the optimal threshold for maximizing F1-score occurs near 0.45-0.50, which aligns well with the default threshold. However, for screening applications where minimizing false negatives is paramount, a lower threshold (around 0.35-0.40) could be employed, accepting more false positives in exchange for catching a higher percentage of diabetic cases.

![Threshold Analysis](evaluation_results/threshold_analysis.png)  
*Figure 4: Performance metrics across different classification thresholds. The analysis reveals that lowering the threshold below 0.5 increases recall (sensitivity) at the expense of precision, allowing clinicians to optimize the trade-off based on specific screening requirements.*

The threshold analysis demonstrates the flexibility of the model for different clinical scenarios. For instance, in a resource-constrained setting where follow-up testing is expensive, a higher threshold (0.55-0.60) might be preferred to maximize precision, ensuring that most positive predictions are true positives. Conversely, in a public health screening campaign where the goal is to identify as many at-risk individuals as possible for lifestyle intervention programs, a lower threshold would capture more potential diabetes cases even if it means some false alarms. This flexibility represents a significant practical advantage over rigid classification systems and allows healthcare providers to tailor the model's behavior to their specific needs and constraints.

## 5. Feature Importance and Clinical Insights

The feature importance analysis (Figure: feature_importance.png) reveals which health indicators most strongly influence the model's predictions, providing valuable clinical insights that align well with established medical knowledge. The analysis shows that General Health (GenHlth), Body Mass Index (BMI), and Age emerge as the top three most important features, collectively accounting for a substantial portion of the model's predictive power. This finding corroborates extensive epidemiological research linking obesity, aging, and overall health status to diabetes risk. The prominence of BMI as a top predictor is particularly reassuring, as this aligns with medical consensus recognizing obesity as one of the most significant modifiable risk factors for Type 2 diabetes.

![Feature Importance](evaluation_results/feature_importance.png)  
*Figure 5: Top 15 feature importances derived from the XGBoost model. General Health, BMI, and Age dominate the predictions, consistent with established medical knowledge about diabetes risk factors.*

High Blood Pressure (HighBP) and High Cholesterol (HighChol) also feature prominently in the importance ranking, reflecting the well-documented connection between cardiovascular health indicators and diabetes risk. These conditions often co-occur as components of metabolic syndrome, and their high importance in our model suggests it has learned these clinically relevant associations. Interestingly, factors such as difficulty walking (DiffWalk) and physical health days (PhysHlth) also contribute meaningfully to predictions, highlighting the model's ability to capture the relationship between physical limitations and diabetes risk. The presence of demographic factors like income and education among the important features reflects known socioeconomic disparities in diabetes prevalence, though care must be taken to ensure these factors don't introduce unwanted bias into clinical decision-making.

The feature importance analysis also reveals some interesting insights about factors that contribute less than might be initially expected. For instance, while heavy alcohol consumption is medically relevant to diabetes risk, it appears relatively lower in the importance ranking compared to other lifestyle factors. This could reflect either lower prevalence of heavy drinking in the dataset or a more complex, non-linear relationship that the model captures through interactions with other features rather than direct importance. Similarly, fruit and vegetable consumption, while clinically recommended for diabetes prevention, show moderate rather than dominant importance, possibly because their effects are more subtle or work synergistically with other dietary and lifestyle factors not fully captured in the survey data.

## 6. Model Limitations and Honest Assessment

While our model demonstrates respectable performance, it is important to acknowledge its limitations with transparency. The accuracy of 67.54%, while reasonable for a real-world medical dataset, means that approximately one in four predictions will be incorrect. In clinical practice, this necessitates that the model be used as a screening and risk assessment tool rather than a definitive diagnostic instrument. The false positive rate of approximately 29% (derived from specificity of 59.80%) implies that roughly three in ten non-diabetic individuals in the test set would be flagged as at-risk. While acceptable for a screening context where follow-up testing can confirm or rule out diabetes, this rate might be problematic in settings where confirmatory testing is resource-intensive or logistically challenging.

The false negative rate of approximately 20% (derived from sensitivity of 78.07%) represents a more concerning limitation, as it means that one in five actual diabetic cases might be missed by the screening tool. For individuals in this group, the missed detection could delay diagnosis and treatment, potentially allowing the disease to progress to more serious stages. This limitation underscores the importance of combining automated risk assessment with clinical judgment and periodic health monitoring. Clinicians should be advised that a negative prediction from the model does not guarantee absence of diabetes, particularly in patients with other strong risk factors or concerning symptoms. Regular re-screening and consideration of additional diagnostic tests remain essential components of comprehensive diabetes management.

Furthermore, our model's performance on the BRFSS dataset, while robust, may not fully generalize to all populations given the survey's methodology and geographic focus on the United States. The telephone-based survey approach might introduce selection bias towards individuals with phone access and willingness to participate in health surveys, potentially underrepresenting disadvantaged populations who are often at higher diabetes risk. The combined BRFSS+PIMA merging applied to the dataset, while useful for training, does not reflect the real-world prevalence of diabetes (which is considerably lower than 50%), meaning that the practical positive predictive value in a general screening population would be lower than our reported precision of 73.16%. Careful calibration would be needed before deploying this model in a true screening setting where the baseline diabetes prevalence might be 10-15% rather than 50%.

## 7. Comparison with Existing Literature

When comparing our results to existing diabetes prediction studies, several important contextual factors must be considered. Many published studies report higher accuracy rates (80-90%) using the PIMA Indian Diabetes dataset, which contains only 768 samples. Our accuracy of 67.54% is achieved on a dataset approximately 195 times larger (150,000 samples), which inherently contains greater diversity and variability. Larger datasets typically yield more conservative performance estimates because they better represent real-world heterogeneity rather than allowing models to overfit to a small, relatively homogeneous sample. In this context, our AUC of 0.7870 compares favorably to many studies that report AUC in the 0.80-0.86 range on the PIMA dataset.

Our approach distinguishes itself through the integration of multiple Explainable AI (XAI) techniques – SHAP, LIME, and Anchors – which are largely absent from most existing diabetes prediction studies. While several recent papers have begun incorporating SHAP for model interpretation, very few combine multiple XAI methods to provide complementary perspectives on model decisions. This multi-method approach addresses a critical gap in medical AI deployment, where clinicians need not just accurate predictions but also understandable explanations they can communicate to patients and incorporate into clinical decision-making. The clinical care plan generation feature, which uses SHAP values to identify modifiable risk factors and generate personalized recommendations, represents a practical innovation that extends beyond typical diabetes prediction systems.

## 8. Explainable AI Integration and Clinical Value

Beyond raw predictive performance, a major contribution of this work lies in the comprehensive integration of explainability techniques that make the model's reasoning transparent and clinically actionable. The SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) implementation provides game-theoretically sound attributions of each feature's contribution to individual predictions. For instance, when the model predicts high diabetes risk for a patient with BMI of 35, SHAP can quantify exactly how much this elevated BMI contributes to the risk score (e.g., +0.12 in log-odds), alongside contributions from other factors like age and blood pressure. This quantitative breakdown allows clinicians to understand not just what the model predicted, but why it made that prediction and which factors are most amenable to intervention.

The LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) integration complements SHAP by providing local linear approximations that are often more intuitive for clinical communication. While SHAP values operate in the model's native decision space (log-odds for binary classification), LIME can present explanations in terms of probability changes that are easier for patients to understand. For example, LIME might explain that "being in the BMI > 30 category increases your diabetes risk by approximately 15 percentage points" – a statement that patients and clinicians can readily grasp. The Anchors implementation further enhances interpretability by generating high-precision if-then rules, such as "IF age ≥ 55 AND BMI ≥ 30 AND high blood pressure THEN diabetes risk is high (96% precision)," which can be incorporated into clinical guidelines and patient education materials.

The care plan generation system leverages these XAI insights to provide personalized, actionable recommendations based on the modifiable risk factors identified by SHAP. Unlike systems that provide generic diabetes prevention advice, our approach specifically targets the factors most responsible for an individual's elevated risk. For a patient whose high risk stems primarily from obesity and physical inactivity, the care plan emphasizes weight management and exercise. For another patient whose risk is driven more by poor general health and cardiovascular factors, recommendations might focus on blood pressure control and overall health improvement. This personalization, grounded in the model's actual reasoning rather than generic heuristics, represents a meaningful advance toward truly precision medicine approaches in diabetes prevention.

## 9. Practical Deployment Considerations

The deployment of this system as a Flask-based web application with user authentication and batch processing capabilities demonstrates a commitment to practical clinical utility rather than remaining a research prototype. The ability to process individual patient records through the self-monitoring interface enables patient-initiated risk assessment, potentially increasing health engagement and early identification of at-risk individuals. The batch processing feature allows healthcare organizations to analyze population-level data, identifying high-risk cohorts for targeted intervention programs. The literature comparison feature provides transparency by benchmarking the system's performance against published studies, helping users contextualize the predictions and understand the system's relative strengths and limitations.

However, several practical challenges must be addressed before widespread clinical deployment. The requirement for 21 input features, while comprehensive, may pose data collection challenges in some clinical settings, particularly for busy primary care practices where detailed health surveys might be time-consuming. The current implementation uses a default value for the Income feature since it was deemed too sensitive for user input, but this necessitates acknowledging that predictions for patients with significantly different socioeconomic profiles than the median might be less accurate. The PDF parsing capability, while innovative, currently requires access to OpenAI's API and has been deliberately stubbed due to concerns about reliability and the mismatch between PIMA-focused extraction patterns and BRFSS features.

Database integration represents another practical consideration for clinical deployment. The current SQLite implementation is suitable for demonstration and small-scale deployment but would need upgrading to a more robust database system (PostgreSQL, MySQL) for handling large numbers of concurrent users in a hospital or health system context. Security and privacy protections would need significant enhancement beyond basic password hashing to comply with healthcare regulations like HIPAA, including audit logging, encrypted data transmission, role-based access control, and secure data storage. The model update mechanism currently requires manually running the training script and replacing model files, which is feasible for periodic retraining but might need automation and version control for production use.

## 10. Future Directions and Improvement Opportunities

Several promising directions exist for enhancing the system's clinical utility and performance. The DiCE (Diverse Counterfactual Explanations) integration, currently stubbed out, represents a high-priority addition that would enable "what-if" scenario generation, showing patients specifically how changing certain modifiable factors (e.g., reducing BMI from 35 to 28, starting regular exercise) would alter their predicted risk. Implementing DiCE requires careful constraint design to ensure suggestions are realistic and actionable, focusing only on truly modifiable features while respecting constraints like biological limits and feasibility.

Temporal modeling represents another valuable enhancement opportunity. The current system processes cross-sectional data, providing a snapshot risk assessment based on current health status. Extending this to track patients longitudinally, monitoring how their risk evolves over time and whether they're successfully modifying risk factors based on care plan recommendations, would greatly enhance clinical value. This would require developing a data model for storing patient histories, implementing trend analysis algorithms, and creating visualizations that show risk trajectories over time. Such temporal tracking could also enable early detection of concerning trends (e.g., steadily increasing BMI or declining general health) before they culminate in diabetes diagnosis.

Ensemble modeling and advanced techniques offer potential, though modest, performance improvements. While our single XGBoost model achieves respectable performance, ensemble approaches combining XGBoost with other algorithms (Random Forest, Neural Networks, Logistic Regression) might capture complementary patterns and improve robustness. However, such improvements would need to be weighed against increased complexity and potential reduction in interpretability. Deep learning approaches, particularly architectures designed for tabular data like TabNet or entity embeddings, have shown promise in some medical applications, though again at the cost of reduced interpretability compared to tree-based models. Any such enhancements would need to preserve the explainability that represents a core strength of the current system.

## 11. Ethical Considerations and Bias Awareness

An honest discussion of model deployment must address potential sources of bias and ethical concerns. The BRFSS dataset, while large and diverse, inherently reflects the demographics and health patterns of U.S. telephone survey respondents, which may not perfectly represent all populations. The inclusion of demographic factors like income and education in the model raises questions about fairness – while these factors have genuine statistical associations with diabetes risk (reflecting socioeconomic health disparities), using them in predictive models risks perpetuating healthcare inequities rather than addressing their root causes. We deliberately chose not to display income in the user interface and use a default value precisely because of these concerns, but this represents an imperfect compromise that warrants ongoing evaluation.

The model's differential performance across subpopulations (which we have not specifically analyzed in this evaluation) could potentially introduce disparities in screening effectiveness. If the model performs significantly better for certain demographic groups than others, it might provide more accurate risk assessment for already well-served populations while being less reliable for disadvantaged groups who might benefit most from early intervention. Future work should include stratified evaluation across age, gender, socioeconomic, and racial/ethnic groups to identify and address any performance disparities. Additionally, the care plan recommendations, while personalized based on individual risk factors, might not adequately account for social determinants of health that affect patients' ability to implement recommended changes (e.g., access to healthy food, safe spaces for exercise, healthcare affordability).

## 12. Conclusion

This diabetes risk prediction system demonstrates that machine learning can achieve clinically useful performance (AUC = 0.7870) while maintaining transparency through comprehensive XAI integration. The accuracy of 67.54%, while modest compared to some published results on smaller datasets, represents honest performance on a large, diverse test set and includes high sensitivity (78.07%) that makes it suitable for screening applications where catching potential cases is paramount. The robust explainability features—SHAP for quantitative attribution, LIME for intuitive local explanations, and Anchors for rule-based clarity—address the longstanding criticism of medical AI as opaque "black boxes" and provide clinicians with tools to understand, validate, and communicate model decisions.

However, deployment of such systems requires careful consideration of their limitations. The approximately 20% false negative rate means that negative predictions should not be treated as definitive clearances, particularly for patients with other risk factors. The 29% false positive rate, while acceptable for screening, necessitates follow-up testing infrastructure to confirm model predictions. Most importantly, the model should be viewed as a decision support tool that augments, rather than replaces, clinical judgment. Physicians and healthcare providers bring contextual knowledge, patient relationship history, and clinical expertise that no model can fully capture. The true value of systems like this lies not in superseding human judgment but in scaling expertise, identifying at-risk individuals who might otherwise be missed, and providing quantitative risk assessment that informs, rather than dictates, clinical decisions.

Looking forward, the integration of temporal tracking, counterfactual generation via DiCE, and more sophisticated handling of social determinants of health could significantly enhance the system's clinical utility. However, technical improvements must be accompanied by careful attention to bias mitigation, health equity considerations, and ethical deployment practices. The moderate but robust performance achieved by this system—honest, transparent, and grounded in realistic evaluation on a large dataset—provides a foundation for iterative improvement and thoughtful clinical integration that prioritizes patient benefit and health equity over pursuit of headline-grabbing accuracy numbers that may not generalize to real-world deployment.
